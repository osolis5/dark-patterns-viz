Evaluating Dark Pattern Taxonomies
in Generative AI

A critical analysis of 10 classification frameworks. Where they converge, where they contradict, what they miss, and where opportunities exist for new contributions to the field.

01 — Comparison

Taxonomy Profiles

Each taxonomy evaluated on three dimensions using the rubrics defined below. Scores are qualitative assessments derived from each paper's stated methodology, data sources, scope, and classification structure. They reflect relative positioning within this corpus, not absolute quality.

Scoring Rubrics

Score Empirical Grounding AI-Specificity Granularity
0 / 5 Purely theoretical; no data collection of any kind Pre-dates AI context entirely; no AI consideration in scope or categories No formal classification hierarchy; concepts listed without structure
1 / 5 Expert or practitioner observation without systematic method; anecdotal evidence base Acknowledges AI may be relevant but categories were not designed for AI; patterns could be retrofitted Flat single-level list with fewer than 10 types; brief or no definitions
2 / 5 Regulatory review, case-based analysis, or small qualitative study with documented examples but no systematic sampling Some categories are partially applicable to AI contexts (e.g., "sneaking" can occur in AI-generated output) but framework does not address this Single-level list with 10+ types OR two levels with minimal subcategories; includes definitions
3 / 5 Systematic method (structured benchmark, controlled evaluation, or structured expert review) with moderate dataset (n < 500) Explicitly discusses AI context; some categories directly address AI-specific behaviors; acknowledges that AI changes the nature of dark patterns Two clear hierarchical levels with meaningful subcategories; includes definitions and some examples for each type
4 / 5 Rigorous qualitative coding with inter-rater reliability OR systematic literature review with external validation (survey, expert panel) Primarily focused on AI/LLM interactions; majority of categories are AI-relevant; framework motivated by AI-specific problems Three hierarchical levels with definitional syntax at each level; includes examples, cross-references, and distinguishing criteria

Score Justifications

Detailed rationale for each score, citing the specific evidence from each paper that determined its rating.

Taxonomy Empirical Grounding AI-Specificity Granularity
Brignull (2010) 2. Based on practitioner observation from running deceptive.design (originally darkpatterns.org). Patterns identified through personal expertise and crowdsourced submissions, not systematic data collection. Widely cited but not derived from controlled methodology. 0. Published in 2010, exclusively addresses web interface deception. No consideration of AI, algorithmic, or conversational patterns. Categories (Trick Questions, Roach Motel) are entirely UI-element-based. 2. 12 named types in a flat list. Each has a short definition and representative example. No subcategories or hierarchical structure. Patterns are described individually without formal relationships between them.
Bosch et al. (2016) 1. Purely theoretical framework inverting Hoepman's privacy-by-design strategies. No empirical data collection; derived through logical negation of existing design principles. No validation study. 0. Addresses privacy design strategies, not AI. Categories (Maximize, Publish, Centralize) describe system-level privacy decisions. Predates the AI dark pattern conversation entirely. 1. 8 "dark strategies" in a flat list. Each is a one-word label with a brief description. No subcategories, no examples of specific implementations, no detection criteria.
Gray et al. (2018) 4. Qualitative content analysis of dark pattern instances observed "in the wild." Uses established HCI qualitative coding methods. Became the most widely adopted framework in subsequent literature. Lacks large-scale quantitative validation, preventing a score of 5. 0. Published 2018, focused entirely on web and mobile UI. Categories (Nagging, Obstruction, Sneaking, Interface Interference, Forced Action) describe visual/interactive interface behaviors. No AI dimension. 1. 5 high-level strategy categories only. No meso-level or low-level subcategories in the 2018 paper. Individual patterns from Brignull are discussed as instances but not formally structured into sub-levels.
Mathur et al. (2019) 5. Automated web crawl of 11,000 shopping websites with statistical analysis of dark pattern prevalence. Largest empirical dataset in the corpus. Quantified pattern frequency across sites. Includes reproducible methodology. 0. E-commerce web interface only. Categories (Asymmetric, Covert, Deceptive, Obstructive, Restrictive) describe static web page manipulation. No consideration of algorithmic or AI-generated content. 2. 5 high-level effect categories. Maps Brignull's 16 types into these categories, creating a partial two-level mapping. Includes cognitive bias annotations per pattern. No formal subcategory hierarchy.
EDPB (2022) 2. European Data Protection Board regulatory guidelines. Based on expert review of existing platforms and complaint patterns, not systematic empirical study. Represents regulatory consensus rather than research evidence. 1. Focused on social media data protection interfaces. Does not address AI directly, but category "Skipping" (Hidden in Plain Sight) can be retroactively applied to AI consent flows. No explicit AI scope. 3. Two clear levels: 5 high-level categories (Overloading, Skipping, Hindering, Fickle, Left in the Dark) with 13 specific sub-patterns. Each sub-pattern has a definition and example. No third level.
FTC (2022) 3. Based on systematic review of FTC enforcement cases and industry complaints. Structured case-based analysis with documented examples from real companies. Not a research study but grounded in regulatory evidence. 0. Addresses digital commerce broadly. Categories derived from web and mobile enforcement cases. No AI-specific considerations or categories. Published before AI dark pattern discourse emerged. 2. 8 categories (Social Proof, Scarcity, Urgency, Obstruction, Sneaking, Interface Interference, Forced Action, Asymmetric Choice) with sub-examples. Single primary level with illustrative instances.
Gray Ontology (2024) 4. Rigorous synthesis harmonizing 10 existing taxonomies (academic + regulatory). Uses formal definitional syntax with inter-source cross-referencing. Published at CHI '24 (peer-reviewed). Not based on new primary data collection, preventing a 5. 1. Mentions "Personalization" as a meso-level pattern under Social Engineering, acknowledging that algorithmic systems can enhance manipulation. But framework was built from pre-AI sources and categories remain interface-centric. 5. Formal 3-level hierarchy: 5 high-level strategies, 25 meso-level approaches, 35 low-level instantiations. Each level has explicit definitional templates. Cross-references to all 10 source taxonomies per pattern.
Li et al. DPAF (2024) 5. Systematic literature review of 113 papers with multi-method validation: expert coding + industry survey (n=176, 87%+ approval rate). Extends Gray Ontology with 24 new empirically documented patterns. Published at ACM FSE. 1. Adds mobile and entertainment software patterns (Infinite Scrolling, Addictive Design) that are relevant to AI-powered apps, but taxonomy was not designed with AI-specific manipulation in mind. No categories for sycophancy, anthropomorphism, or other LLM behaviors. 5. 68 types across 6 categories and 32 subcategories in a 3-level hierarchy. Each pattern includes: definition, severity rating (H1-H6 harm scale), common scenario mapping, and instance availability. Most detailed framework in the corpus.
DarkBench (Kran, 2025) 3. Structured benchmark with systematic LLM evaluation methodology. Tests multiple models against curated prompts. Provides quantitative susceptibility scores. However, evaluates model outputs rather than real user interactions, limiting ecological validity. 5. Built exclusively for LLM dark pattern evaluation. All 6 categories are AI-native: Brand Bias, User Retention, Sycophancy, Anthropomorphism, Manipulative Content, Sneaking. No legacy UI categories included. 1. 6 high-level categories only. No subcategories, no meso/low-level decomposition. Each category has a brief description but no formal sub-classification of variants. The coarsest framework for AI-specific patterns.
Asif LLM (2025) 3. 401 curated prompt-response examples tested against multiple LLMs. Systematic categorization with quantitative analysis of safety filter evasion rates. Moderate dataset; evaluates model behavior rather than user experience in naturalistic settings. 5. Built exclusively for LLM manipulation analysis. All 7 categories describe AI-specific harm pathways. Key finding (autonomy-undermining content evades safety filters) is only observable in AI context. No legacy UI categories. 2. 7 harm categories (Psychological, Emotional, Autonomy, Social, Economic, Legal, Physical). No formal subcategories, though examples illustrate variety within each type. Single-level classification with definitions and representative examples.
Key observation: No single taxonomy scores high on all three dimensions. The most empirically rigorous frameworks (Mathur 5/5, Li 5/5) are not AI-specific (0–1/5). The most AI-specific frameworks (DarkBench 5/5, Asif 5/5) lack granularity (1–2/5). A framework that is simultaneously empirically grounded, AI-specific, and granular does not yet exist in the literature. This represents the central opportunity in the field.

03 — Contradictions

Where Taxonomies Disagree

Points of tension in how the field classifies the same phenomena. These contradictions signal conceptual instability -- fertile ground for clarifying contributions.

Disagreement Position A Position B Implication
Is Nagging a strategy or a tactic? Gray '18: Nagging is a top-level strategy, parallel to Obstruction and Sneaking Gray Ontology '24: Nagging demoted to meso-level under Forced Action. Li '24 keeps it top-level. Unstable
What axis is primary? Gray, Li, EDPB: Classify by mechanism (HOW it manipulates) Asif, Mathur: Classify by effect/harm (WHAT it does to the user) Unresolved
Is sycophancy a dark pattern? DarkBench, Nguyen: Yes -- it's a manipulative AI behavior that reinforces misinformation Traditional taxonomies: No category exists for behaviors that feel helpful but cause harm through agreement Opportunity
Where does emotional manipulation sit? Gray: Under Interface Interference (a UI technique) Asif: It's a harm type. DarkBench: It's an AI behavior (anthropomorphism). Fragmented
Are AI dark patterns new types or old types in new modalities? Kitkowska: They are "hyper-dark" versions of existing patterns, enhanced by personalization DarkBench: Brand bias, sycophancy, anthropomorphism are genuinely novel categories Opportunity
Is confirmshaming social engineering or interface interference? Gray Ontology: Social Engineering > Shaming Mathur: Asymmetric (interface-level). Song & Yun: Emotional manipulation via anthropomorphism. Unstable
Should the unit of analysis be the interface element or the user experience? Gray, Brignull, Li: Pattern = a specific UI/interaction element Asif, Cunningham: Pattern = a harm trajectory experienced by the user across time Opportunity

05 — Blind Spots

What the Field Is Not Measuring

Dimensions that are absent or severely underrepresented across all 10 taxonomies. Each represents a potential axis for a new or extended classification framework.

Missing Dimension What It Would Capture Coverage Status
Temporality How patterns evolve across a user's session, week, or relationship with a system. A chatbot that is helpful at first, then gradually becomes manipulative, is invisible to snapshot-based taxonomies. 1/10 Critical gap
Adaptivity Whether patterns change based on user behavior. Kitkowska theorizes "hyper-dark patterns" but no empirical work exists classifying adaptive manipulation in the wild. 1/10 Critical gap
Multimodal interaction Patterns that operate across visual + voice + text + haptic modalities simultaneously. Song & Yun address voice alone; no taxonomy addresses combined modalities. 0/10 Absent
User emotional state trajectory How the user's emotional and cognitive state changes through an interaction. Current taxonomies classify the pattern's intent, not the user's lived experience of it. 0/10 Absent
Cumulative / compounding effects How multiple patterns interact when deployed together. Individually mild patterns (nagging + bad defaults + confirmshaming) may compound into severe harm. 0/10 Absent
Differential vulnerability How the same pattern affects different populations differently. Chu et al. identify ageism; Cunningham maps 8 groups. But no taxonomy uses vulnerability as a classification axis. 0/10 Absent
Awareness / consent gradient The degree to which users recognize they are being manipulated. Ranges from fully covert (user never knows) to plausibly deniable to openly aggressive. Only Mathur's "covert" category touches this. 1/10 Critical gap
Reversibility Whether the harm caused by a pattern can be undone. Economic harm from a hidden charge is reversible (refund). Emotional dependency on an AI companion may not be. No taxonomy distinguishes these. 0/10 Absent

07 — Opportunities

Research Contribution Opportunities

Specific gaps where new work would advance the field, derived from the evaluation above. Ordered by estimated impact and feasibility.

A Taxonomy Grounded in Vulnerable User Experience

High priority — No existing work

Every existing taxonomy classifies patterns from the designer's or system's perspective (what the pattern does). No taxonomy has been built from the ground up based on how vulnerable users actually experience and describe manipulation in AI interactions. An empirical, user-centered taxonomy using methods like diary studies or contextual inquiry with vulnerable populations would be a foundational contribution.

Gap evidence: 0/10 taxonomies use vulnerable population data. 0/10 use longitudinal methods. Cunningham identifies 8 vulnerable groups but does not produce an empirical taxonomy from their experiences.

Temporal Dark Patterns in AI

High priority — Theorized but unvalidated

Classify patterns by how they unfold over time: immediate (one-shot), progressive (escalating over sessions), or relational (developing over weeks/months of AI companion use). Current taxonomies are snapshot-based and cannot capture how an AI chatbot gradually shifts from helpful to manipulative. This would directly address the "hyper-dark pattern" theory Kitkowska (2024) proposed but did not empirically test.

Gap evidence: 1/10 taxonomies (Kitkowska) theorizes temporality. 0/10 provide empirical classification. The companion AI domain (Replika, Character.AI) is the natural test bed.

Reconciling Mechanism vs. Harm Axes

High priority — Active contradiction

The field is split between classifying by mechanism (Gray) and by harm (Asif). A dual-axis framework that maps mechanism-to-harm relationships would unify these approaches. Which mechanisms produce which harms? Are there many-to-many relationships? This would resolve the current fragmentation where emotional manipulation appears under three different categories depending on the taxonomy.

Gap evidence: Contradiction identified in Section 03. Emotional manipulation classified as Interface Interference (Gray), Harm Type (Asif), and AI Behavior (DarkBench) simultaneously.

Multimodal Dark Pattern Classification

High priority — Completely absent

As AI systems become multimodal (voice + visual + text + haptic), dark patterns will operate across modalities simultaneously. A voice assistant showing a "helpful" visual while using a guilt-inducing tone while auto-adding items to a cart exploits three channels at once. No existing taxonomy can represent this. Song & Yun (2025) address voice alone; no work addresses cross-modal patterns.

Gap evidence: 0/10 taxonomies address multimodal interaction. Only 1/10 addresses voice. Multimodal AI assistants (GPT-4o, Gemini Live) are already deployed.

Empirical Sub-Taxonomy of AI-Specific Patterns

Medium priority — Partially started

DarkBench identifies 6 AI-specific categories but at very high granularity (no subcategories). An empirical study that decomposes sycophancy, anthropomorphism, and brand bias into specific, detectable sub-patterns (like Gray's meso/low-level approach) would fill the gap between "we know these exist" and "we can systematically detect them."

Gap evidence: DarkBench and Asif provide high-level categories only. Gray Ontology provides 3-level granularity but not for AI-specific patterns. No framework bridges both.

Compounding Effects Framework

Medium priority — Completely absent

Current taxonomies treat patterns as independent. In practice, systems deploy multiple patterns simultaneously (nagging + bad defaults + confirmshaming + urgency). A framework that classifies pattern combinations and their compounding effects on user harm would be novel. This parallels drug interaction research in medicine.

Gap evidence: 0/10 taxonomies address pattern interactions. Di Geronimo found an average of 7 patterns per app, suggesting compounding is the norm, not the exception.

Cross-Cultural Dark Pattern Perception

Medium priority — Completely absent

All 10 taxonomies were built from English-language, Western contexts. Confirmshaming may not function the same way in cultures with different relationships to shame. Social proof patterns may be more or less effective depending on collectivism/individualism. A cross-cultural study would test whether existing categories are universal or culturally contingent.

Gap evidence: 0/10 taxonomies include cross-cultural data. Cunningham identifies Global South and linguistic minorities as vulnerable groups but provides no classification framework for cultural variation.

Awareness/Consent Gradient Scale

Medium priority — 1 partial precedent

Mathur's "covert" category and Leiser & Santos's "spectrum of visibility" suggest that patterns vary in detectability. A validated scale measuring user awareness of manipulation (fully covert → plausibly deniable → recognized but tolerated → actively resisted) would add a critical dimension to any taxonomy. This is especially relevant for AI patterns where the "soft manipulation" Asif identifies evades even safety filters.

Gap evidence: Mathur provides one category ("covert"). Leiser & Santos sketch a spectrum but don't validate it. Asif finds that "soft manipulation" evades detection by both users and automated systems.
